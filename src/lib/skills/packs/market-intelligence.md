# Market & Competitive Intelligence Playbook

Map the category before positioning. The goal: find the angle rivals can't copy and the words customers already use. Honest comparison beats hype; evaluators verify claims.

## Map the competitive set (three tiers)
- Direct: solves the same job the same way.
- Adjacent: solves it a different way (a different tool category, a broader suite).
- Status quo: spreadsheets, manual work, doing nothing. Often the real competitor.
For each, note one strength, one weakness, and the genuine edge this product has. Differentiate on what customers value, not feature parity.

## Profile each rival
- Positioning and who they target.
- Pricing model and which tier gives the core value.
- Common complaints (mine review language: G2, Reddit, app stores).
- "Best for" and "not for."
Pull the verbatim phrases. Customer complaints about a rival are your sharpest messaging angles.

## Find the differentiated angle
1. List rivals' shared claims (the category cliches everyone says).
2. Strike anything a rival also says. What survives is your wedge.
3. Reframe a perceived weakness as a deliberate trade-off ("not for enterprise" = "built for solo founders").
4. Pick the segment that values your wedge MOST and aim there.
Example: every scheduler says "save time." Wedge = "the only one that writes the post too," aimed at solopreneurs who hate the blank page.

## Honest comparison rules
- Acknowledge rival strengths; it builds trust and reads as credible.
- State who each option is best for, including when a rival wins.
- Explain WHY a difference matters, with a use case, not just a checkmark.
- Never misrepresent a competitor; the reader is comparing in another tab.

## Category positioning choices
- Head-to-head: win an existing category on a clear axis.
- Niche: "X for Y" (e.g. "Notion for wedding planners"). Smaller, easier to own.
- New category: name a new problem. High payoff, high cost of education.
Anchor to a macro trend that makes now the right time to care.

## Moats (what keeps the edge)
Network effects (better as more join), switching costs (painful to leave), proprietary data, scale economics, brand trust. Name which one applies; if none yet, differentiate on focus and customer love.

## Anti-patterns
- Comparing on feature count instead of customer-valued outcomes.
- Trashing rivals; it signals insecurity and invites fact-checking.
- Claiming "no real competition" (status quo always competes).
- Generic angles every rival already owns.
- Inflated market claims with no segment focus.
